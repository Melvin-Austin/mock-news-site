STORY 4 — Indigenous Water Rights in Canada (Human Rights Story)
Approx. 1–2 minutes

[Tone shift — more serious here. Travis holds back on jokes.]
[Show graphic: clean water imagery or First Nations community photo]

AUSTIN: Our final story today is one that we think every Canadian needs to know about — and frankly, one that should make all of us uncomfortable. Right now, in 2026, there are 39 long-term drinking water advisories in effect across 37 First Nations communities in Canada. That means people cannot safely drink water out of their taps.
TRAVIS: What its its 2026 come on guys we gotta get better at all this. 
AUSTIN: In Canada. In 2026. According to Indigenous Services Canada, some of these advisories have been in place for over thirty years. Entire generations of Indigenous children have grown up never knowing what it's like to just turn on a tap and drink the water safely.
TRAVIS: Every single person watching this has done that today. Multiple times. Without even thinking about it.
AUSTIN: Exactly. And this isn't just a logistical problem — it's a human rights issue. The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, which Canada has signed, recognizes the right to clean water as a fundamental human right. The Canadian government under former Prime Minister Justin Trudeau promised to end all boil water advisories by 2021. That deadline passed. Many communities still wait.
TRAVIS: So a promise was made. And people are still waiting hm that’s interesting...
AUSTIN: Canada's own Auditor General rated the government's progress on this issue as — and I'm quoting here — 'unsatisfactory' in 2025. The current federal government under Prime Minister Mark Carney has promised new legislation, but as the Council of Canadians notes, there's been very little sense of urgency. The federal government has also been criticized for under funding water system maintenance on reserves by an estimated 138 million dollars per year according to the Parliamentary Budget Officer.
TRAVIS: 138 million. The government found billions for other things. But clean water for Indigenous communities keeps getting delayed everyone should have fresh clean water we need it.
AUSTIN: This connects directly to Section 35 of the Canadian Constitution, which recognizes and affirms Indigenous rights, and to the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples. The people most affected are represented — or supposed to be represented — by First Nations leadership, Indigenous Services Canada, and ultimately, all of us as Canadian citizens who elect the governments making these decisions.
TRAVIS: That last part is on us. Genuinely.
AUSTIN: It really is. This is what civic responsibility looks like — understanding that rights on paper mean nothing if the systems to protect them aren't funded and enforced. Alright — let's wrap up.
